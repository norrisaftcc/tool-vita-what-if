# Teaching Students to Figure It Out — Literally
## A formulation-first initiative in Computer Programming & Development

**Briefing for: College Administration**
**From: Computer Programming & Development**
**Re: FIGURE — a measurable self-reliance curriculum, and the in-house AI assistant built to enforce it**

---

Here is a scene that repeats in every technical program we run, and its cost is invisible because it has always been there. A student arrives at office hours and says, "my code doesn't work." What follows is fifteen or twenty minutes in which a faculty member extracts, question by question, the basic facts the student didn't bring: what the program actually did, what the student expected it to do, whether it ever worked, what the error message says. Only after that excavation does any teaching begin. Multiply by every student, every section, every week, and you find that the scarcest resource in the college — faculty attention — is being spent primarily on information recovery, not instruction. The forum posts, the emails, and increasingly the students' own prompts to AI chatbots all fail the same way, because they are all the same failure: the student cannot yet *specify a problem*.

That reframing turned out to be the most valuable thing our department has learned in several years, and we learned it the honest way. Our first-generation AI teaching assistant — VITA, built and hosted entirely on college hardware to keep student data in-house — was designed to answer debugging questions, and it did. But watching it in use showed us we had built a good answer to the wrong question. Students who couldn't specify their problems got poor help from any assistant, human or artificial, and unlimited access to an answering tutor gave them unlimited practice at the very dependency we were trying to cure. So we went back to the drawing board, this time with the problem stated correctly — and the result is a curriculum, an assessment design, and a rebuilt assistant that together cost less than what they replace.

The curriculum is called FIGURE: six fixed diagnostic moves a student runs on their own stuck problem before asking anyone for help — human, forum, or AI. The approach is not novel and that is its strength; it is the classroom application of some of the oldest, best-replicated findings in learning science. Polya built modern mathematics teaching on exactly such fixed questions in 1945. The self-explanation effect (Chi et al., 1989) — the finding behind the folk practice programmers call "rubber-duck debugging" — shows that precisely articulating a problem frequently solves it outright. And cognitive apprenticeship (Collins, Brown & Newman, 1989) supplies the delivery method: faculty model the same questions relentlessly for weeks, and only when students begin answering before being asked do we hand over the named system. From that day forward, "figure it out" stops being the most dismissive sentence in education and becomes a literal assignment: six moves, in order, each with a checkable result.

The rebuilt assistant enforces the practice. It asks the FIGURE questions and is constructed to do nothing else — it cannot write the student's code, name the bug, or hint at the fix, even when the answer is obvious to it. We would draw your attention to what this does to the college's AI story. While institutions everywhere are caught between policing student AI use and surrendering to it, this is a third position: an AI tool that *cannot do the work for the student*, that runs entirely on hardware the college already owns, that sends no student data to any outside company, and whose entire design is to make the student more capable of working without it. It is difficult to imagine a cleaner answer to "what is responsible AI in the classroom" than an assistant whose success condition is the student no longer needing it — and whose finest sessions, in practice, are the ones where the student solves the problem in the middle of describing it.

What makes this an initiative rather than a teaching style is that we can measure it, in language built for outcome catalogs and accreditors: *students will formulate falsifiable problem statements.* That sentence is also, word for word, a description of what industry calls writing a competent bug ticket — this is a directly employable professional behavior, and it happens to be the same skill that determines whether a graduate can use workplace AI tools productively. One outcome, three payoffs: self-reliance in our courses, a résumé-legible workplace skill, and transferable AI fluency. We score help requests on a six-criterion rubric all semester, and the rubric has a property worth savoring: a student who tries to game it — mechanically filling in the literal output, the hand-computed expectation, the last working version — has performed the debugging work in the act of gaming. The measurement is the intervention.

At semester's end you receive four charts, not testimonials: formulation quality trending up across the term; the gap closing between how students perform with an instructor present and alone (the difference between compliance and genuine internalization — we designed the measurement specifically to catch the failure mode where students merely learn to satisfy the professor); faculty minutes per resolved issue falling as requests arrive already specified; and the *withdrawn-ticket rate* — the fraction of help requests abandoned mid-writing with "never mind, found it," which is self-reliance caught on camera. One quieter benefit belongs in this list: because every student periodically submits structured help requests — including about problems they solved themselves — the students who currently struggle in silence and surface only at the drop deadline become visible in week three instead. We will also tell you plainly what these numbers cannot claim: with no control section, this is evidence that the outcome was achieved and internalized, not a controlled trial against alternatives. We would rather under-claim to you now than over-claim and be caught later.

The resource request is the best part of the memo: there isn't one. The assistant's questions-only design is what allows it to run on small models on existing department hardware, at no marginal cost per student and with no vendor contract — the pedagogically correct version turned out to be the inexpensive version, which is not a sentence one gets to write often. What we ask instead is awareness and endorsement: this will look unusual in classroom observations (an instructor conspicuously *not* answering, an AI conspicuously refusing to help), and we want you to recognize that as the design working. Our first tool taught us the real problem statement. This is the tool — and the curriculum — we originally meant to build.
