# The Stall

It is 11:40 on a Tuesday night and the lab is empty except for Marisol, one humming vending machine, and a cursor. The cursor blinks whether or not you are ready, which is the first honest thing most programs ever do. She has typed *it's broken* into the terminal, because it is broken, and because at 11:40 on a Tuesday that is all the language she has left.

The terminal considers this for the amount of time a small machine takes to pretend to consider things, and asks: **Broken how? What did it do, and what did you expect it to do?**

She does not answer right away. Instead — and you know how this happens, late at night, when a question hangs there long enough to become a mirror — she remembers week two.

---

Week two, the instructor had stood at the front of the lab while eleven programs failed in eleven different ways, and had answered none of them. He had a way of being interested in a bug the way a mechanic is interested in a noise, which was infuriating, until it wasn't. And that day, instead of helping, he told them about his first job.

"I worked one summer for a mechanic named Ordoñez," he said, "who never touched a car until the owner did something first. I didn't understand it for a long time. You will understand it before I finish telling you — most people do."

---

Ordoñez's garage had two bays and a coffee pot older than the county. A man brought in a sedan and said: *it makes a noise.*

Ordoñez did not open the hood. He poured coffee and asked what kind of noise. The man said *a bad one*, and Ordoñez nodded the way you nod at weather, and asked what the car sounded like on the mornings it *didn't* make the noise, because a man who has heard the bad noise has also, whether he has noticed yet or not, heard its absence, and knows the border between them better than anyone alive.

The man started to answer and slowed down halfway through, the way people slow down when the sentence they are building starts building something back. "It's only in the mornings," he said. "Only when it's cold. It goes away by the highway." He was quiet for a moment, listening to his own description as if someone smarter had said it. "It's the belt," he said. "It's the belt when it's cold, isn't it."

Ordoñez asked the only question he ever asked at that point in a conversation: "What would prove it?"

"Warm it in the bay first," the man said slowly. "If it starts quiet, it's the belt."

They warmed it in the bay. It started quiet. Ordoñez charged him for a belt and for the coffee, and the man drove off having fixed, in every way that matters, his own car. The instructor said the important part was this: Ordoñez knew it was the belt from the parking lot. Knowing was never the job.

---

The instructor didn't finish the story that day — the fire alarm got tested, or the projector died, or he simply stopped, and Marisol could never afterward remember which, only that the story sat open in her for five more weeks like a program paused on a breakpoint. He closed it during the lecture in week seven, the one where he finally put the six letters on the board and said: *you've noticed I ask the same questions every time; you've started answering them before I ask; here they are, named, and they're yours now.* Then: "Ordoñez's trick was that there was no trick. He asked what happens, compared to what, and when it doesn't — and then he held still. You now know everything he knew. So when I tell you to figure it out, I am not sending you away. I am handing you the garage."

---

At 11:44 the cursor is still blinking. Marisol starts typing what she expected the output to be, literally, for this input, computed by hand — and stops on the fourth character.

Because before you find it — and she was about to find it — there is a half-second where the expected value and the code hold still next to each other, and the border between them turns visible, the way a noise teaches you what quiet was. Her loop starts at one. It has always started at one. It has been skipping the first element the whole time, politely, in plain sight.

She types: *never mind. found it.*

The terminal, which was built by people who consider this outcome its finest work, asks one more thing anyway, because the contract is the contract:

**What one test would prove that?**

She tells it. She runs it. It passes. The vending machine hums approval, at its usual frequency, for its usual reasons.

---

## Coda, with the hood open

You have just been run through three nested loops — a lab, a classroom, a garage — opened in that order and closed in reverse, which is an old hypnotist's structure for making a middle story do its work while your attention is holding the outer ones open. The piece is seeded with presuppositions ("you will understand it before I finish," "before you find it — and she was about to find it") that assume the outcome inside the grammar, where arguing with it is inconvenient. It met you in a mechanic's metaphor instead of a debugger's, because meeting a mind in its active representation is cheaper than demanding a translation. Every one of these techniques was used on you deliberately, and here they are, named.

Now notice: knowing this, you can read it again and it still works. The story survives its own disclosure.

That is the entire test. Anything that survives being explained to the person it's working on is a tool, and you can hand it over. Anything that dies when named was never teaching — it was only ever a trick.

Ordoñez knew it was the belt from the parking lot.

Knowing was never the job.
