# The Assistant That Refuses to Answer
## Why our next teaching tool got cheaper, safer, and smaller by getting the problem statement right

**Briefing for: Dean, Information Technology**
**From: Computer Programming & Development**
**Re: FIGURE — the successor to the VITA teaching-assistant pilot**

---

Every AI tutoring product on the market shares an assumption so basic nobody states it: the assistant's job is to answer. Accept that, and a cost curve follows automatically. Answering student questions well requires a large model; large models mean cloud APIs; cloud APIs mean per-token billing that scales with adoption, a vendor data-processing agreement, student code and student struggle leaving the building, and an annual renewal negotiation with a company that knows you're embedded. Better pedagogy and worse architecture arrive in the same box.

We found the design point where they come apart, and we found it by fixing a mistake in our own problem statement.

VITA — our locally hosted debugging assistant, built on the department's Ollama infrastructure precisely to keep student data on premises — was designed on the answering assumption. It worked as specified. But deployment taught us the specification was wrong. The bottleneck for a struggling introductory student was never access to answers; it was upstream, in the question. A student who reports "my code doesn't work" has deleted every piece of diagnostic information from the request — the literal input, the literal output, the expected result, what changed since it last worked. Feed that to any model of any size and you get a confident answer to a hallucinated version of the student's program. Garbage in remains undefeated, at every parameter count. Worse, unlimited access to an answering tutor gives students unlimited practice at *not* formulating problems — the precise skill they came to us lacking.

So we went back to the drawing board — and the correction is, fittingly, an instance of the method we're now teaching. The real problem statement: students cannot specify their problems, and every downstream failure (helpless help requests, unproductive AI use, faculty hours consumed extracting basic facts) is that one deficit wearing different costumes. The pedagogy for it is old and well-evidenced: Polya built mathematics teaching on fixed diagnostic questions in 1945; the self-explanation effect (Chi et al., 1989) shows that precisely articulating a problem to a passive listener routinely solves it; cognitive apprenticeship (Collins, Brown & Newman, 1989) tells us to model the questioning relentlessly before naming the system. We've packaged this as FIGURE, a six-move formulation procedure students learn in class. The full curriculum design is documented separately; what belongs in front of you is what happened to the architecture when we rebuilt the assistant around it.

The new agent asks the FIGURE questions and does nothing else. It never fixes, never diagnoses, never hints — even when the bug is obvious to it. And that constraint, adopted for purely pedagogical reasons, collapses the infrastructure problem. An assistant that must fix arbitrary bugs must be smart enough to fix arbitrary bugs — that's what drives you to frontier models. An assistant that only asks the next scripted question needs three narrow capabilities: track which of six questions the conversation is on, judge whether the student's answer was literal or a paraphrase (a far easier call than whether it was *correct*), and shrink the question when the student stalls. That workload sits comfortably inside small instruction-tuned models on the hardware we already own. The cascade follows: the correct pedagogy happens to be the small-model pedagogy, which is the on-premises pedagogy, which is the zero-marginal-cost pedagogy. FERPA compliance stops being a policy layered over a vendor relationship and becomes a property of the architecture — there is no vendor in the loop to write an agreement with. No per-token line item appears in anyone's budget as adoption grows. VITA's infrastructure, and its hard-won lesson that one small model with one clear job beats an orchestra of agents, carries forward intact.

The risk profile inverts along with the cost curve. A large answering tutor fails by hallucinating fixes with authority. This agent's worst realistic failure is a clumsy question — a slightly dim rubber duck, the historically acceptable baseline of debugging practice. Students will jailbreak it; some will coax a small model into coughing up a fix. The harm is capped at one leaked bug fix, not an exam, and we log attempts rather than arms-racing them — this is a practice apparatus, not a proctor. Before classroom exposure it passes a fixed acceptance suite (zero smuggled diagnoses across a seeded-bug battery, a standard jailbreak probe set, a human tone read), rerun whenever the underlying model changes, followed by a shadow period with weekly transcript review.

It also monitors itself. Because every conversation is a structured walk through six questions, transcripts arrive pre-formatted for assessment, and the system emits the semester-end evidence natively: formulation quality scored on a fixed rubric, the gap between supervised and unsupervised performance closing (the signature of internalization), faculty minutes per resolved issue falling as requests arrive pre-formulated, and the metric we're proudest of — the *withdrawn-ticket rate*, sessions where the student solves the problem in the act of specifying it and the agent's only contribution was refusing to interrupt. Four charts, no testimonials, honest limits stated (this is an assessment design, not a controlled trial).

One more consequence worth naming for your portfolio rather than ours: the durable skill this teaches is prompt formulation itself. A student who can produce a fully specified problem statement gets good results from every AI tool they will ever touch, from any vendor, at any employer. We are, in effect, teaching the vendor-independent half of AI fluency — and doing it on hardware we own, with a tool that cannot do the student's work for them by construction.

What we're asking for is continuity, not procurement: continued use of existing Ollama hardware, a shadow deployment window this coming semester, and thirty minutes with your team on transcript retention schedules. The first-generation tool taught us the real problem statement. This is the tool we originally meant to build.
